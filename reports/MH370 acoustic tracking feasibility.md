# Physics mutes acoustic tracking of MH370

The verdict is no. You cannot run the November 2026 aircraft-Doppler technique "in reverse" to retrace MH370's southern flight or its final minutes, and no public sensor archive is placed to do it. Reversing the idea, so that microphones or seismometers at known positions are used to recover an aircraft's track, is old and sound physics. It works for low aircraft heard by sensors a few kilometres away. It fails for a Boeing 777 cruising at about 35,000 ft over open ocean at night. The ground footprint of a cruising jet's sound is only about 25–40 km either side of its track. Hydrophones hear an overflying aircraft only inside a cone roughly 2–3 km wide. No public microphone, seismometer or hydrophone sat inside either footprint along the roughly six hours after military radar lost the aircraft at 18:22 UTC. The final minutes also lack the steady engine tone the method depends on, because both engines had most likely run out of fuel before the 00:19 UTC satellite log-on.

The only acoustic route that could still change the search is a different technique: locating the ocean impact from where and when hydroacoustic signals arrived. That work exists. It has produced three distinct and mutually incompatible candidate events: a ~00:52–00:54 UTC arrival at Cape Leeuwin on bearing ~306°, a ~01:32–01:35 UTC "Curtin event" later traced to the Carlsberg Ridge, and Ed Anderson's 01:15:18 UTC event off Java. None is confirmed. The decisive data are held by the CTBTO behind an institutional contract.

The official search status as of 5 November 2026 is unchanged. Malaysia has extended Ocean Infinity's "no find, no fee" contract to 30 June 2027, about 7,429 km² of the 15,000 km² target remains unsurveyed, and the calm-season window that opened this month has no announced restart date. The useful open-data work is therefore validation, and testing those three candidates against public seismic and IMOS records. It is not a new track reconstruction.

## Twelve years of searching have shrunk MH370 to an arc, not a point

### The official record ends at 18:22 UTC

MH370 left Kuala Lumpur at 16:42 UTC on 7 March 2014 with 239 people aboard. It passed waypoint IGARI at 17:20:31 UTC, and its transponder was gone by about 17:21 UTC ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_370)). Malaysian military primary radar then followed it back across the peninsula, south of Penang at about 17:52 UTC, and up the Strait of Malacca. The **last radar point was at 18:22:12 UTC, about 10 NM beyond waypoint MEKAR** ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf); [MiGFlug](https://migflug.com/afterburner/mh370-what-is-known-2026-ocean-infinity-search/)). The raw military radar has never been released ([radiantphysics](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/?p=920)).

Everything after 18:22 UTC is inferred from Inmarsat metadata. The aircraft's satellite terminal logged back on at 18:25:27 UTC and answered hourly handshakes until 00:10:58 UTC. At **00:19:29 UTC it sent its own log-on request**, which defines the "7th arc". It did not answer the handshake at 01:15:56 UTC ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf)). Burst timing offsets (BTO) give the range from the satellite, so each handshake fixes a ring. Burst frequency offsets (BFO) give a Doppler-like signal that chose the southern route over the northern one ([Ashton et al., J. Navigation 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S037346331400068X)). The ATSB reads the final log-on as an APU-powered restart after fuel exhaustion. Its BFOs imply descent rates of **2,900–15,200 ft/min, rising to 13,800–25,300 ft/min eight seconds later** ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf)).

The DSTG combined these data with a radar prior and a stochastic aircraft-dynamics model in a Bayesian particle filter. The work is published open access as *Bayesian Methods in the Search for MH370* ([OAPEN](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/27976)). Inmarsat's own paper warns of "significant uncertainty". Its sensitivity runs to about **±9° of latitude and ±28° of heading** ([Wikipedia summary of Ashton et al.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_370_satellite_communications)). A March 2026 independent analysis argues that allowing even one autopilot "direct-to" waypoint command widens the range of 7th-arc latitudes consistent with the data ([mh370search.com](https://www.mh370search.com/2026/03/15/where-is-mh370/)).

The searches followed that uncertainty up and down the arc:

- **Surface and pinger searches, March–April 2014.** Neither found anything.
- **ATSB sonar search, 2014–2017.** It covered about **120,000 km² between roughly 32.8°S and 39.5°S** for about A$198 million and was suspended on 17 January 2017 ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf); [ATSB overview](https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview)).
- **First Principles Review, December 2016, and the ATSB final report, 3 October 2017.** Both pointed to an unsearched area of under 25,000 km² to the north. The final report called the loss "almost inconceivable and certainly societally unacceptable" ([APO](https://apo.org.au/node/112656)).
- **CSIRO drift modelling.** It favoured about **35.6°S, 92.8°E** ([CSIRO drift reports](https://www.atsb.gov.au/csiro-mh370-drift-reports)).
- **Debris.** More than 20 items, led by the Réunion flaperon confirmed by serial number in September 2015, place the end in the southern Indian Ocean. They do not give a point ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_370)).
- **Malaysian Safety Investigation Report, July 2018.** It found the turn-back was flown manually but concluded: "the Team is unable to determine the real cause" ([MOT report PDF](https://www.mot.gov.my/en/MH370%20Investigation%20Report/01-Report/MH370SafetyInvestigationReport.pdf); [MiGFlug](https://migflug.com/afterburner/mh370-what-is-known-2026-ocean-infinity-search/)).

The 2017 final report also delivered a verdict on acoustics. At the First Principles Review, participants concluded that hydroacoustic analysis "did not contribute any useful new information to the search" ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf)).

### Ocean Infinity's three campaigns have found nothing, and a fourth window is open

Ocean Infinity searched north of the ATSB zone from January to May 2018 and covered more than 112,000 km² ([Wikipedia: Search for MH370](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_for_Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_370)). Malaysia signed a new contract on 25 March 2025 with these terms:

- 15,000 km² of new area;
- **US$70 million payable only if the wreck is found**;
- an 18-month term.

Sources: [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/malaysia-extends-search-missing-flight-mh370-by-one-year-2026-06-29/); [MOT update to families](https://www.mot.gov.my/en/Kenyataan%20Media/Year%202026/MH370%20Search%20Operation%202025-2026%20-%20Update%20to%20Families.pdf).

Phase 1 ran from 25 to 28 March 2025. Phase 2, using the Armada 86 05 and its AUVs, ran from 31 December 2025 to 23 January 2026. The two phases together took **28 operational days and surveyed about 7,571 km², with no finding**. That is the official tally in the AAIB's 8 March 2026 update ([MOT](https://www.mot.gov.my/en/Kenyataan%20Media/Year%202026/MH370%20Search%20Operation%202025-2026%20-%20Update%20to%20Families.pdf)). Ocean Infinity's chief executive said: "we can say with confidence that it isn't where we looked". The company says it has mapped more than 140,000 km² since 2018 ([Ocean Infinity](https://oceaninfinity.com/news/conclusion-of-the-search-for-malaysian-airlines-flight-mh370/)).

Families' group Voice370 pressed for an extension ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/families-flight-mh370-passengers-urge-malaysia-extend-search-2026-03-08/)). On 29 June 2026 Malaysia extended the contract to **30 June 2027, on unchanged terms, to finish the remaining 7,428.54 km²**. Redeployment is possible only "from this November until April 2027" ([The Star/Bernama](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/nation/2026/06/29/govt-extends-deal-with-ocean-infinity-to-search-for-mh370-wreckage)).

As of 5 November 2026, this research found no official Ministry of Transport or Ocean Infinity announcement of a restart date or of a vessel departing. A late-August secondary source said work was "not expected to resume before November" ([MiGFlug](https://migflug.com/afterburner/mh370-what-is-known-2026-ocean-infinity-search/)). The CAPTION group also refers to a possible phase between November 2026 and April 2027 ([CAPTION](https://mh370-caption.net/)). The 2025–26 coordinates have not been published.

### Independent analysts span from 8°S to 38°S

The independent field tells the same story of an arc without a point. The Independent Group has moved its estimate several times:

- **17 June 2014:** about 36°S ([duncansteel.com archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20150323130707/http://www.duncansteel.com/archives/826)).
- **February 2020 (UGIB paper by Iannello, Ulich, Godfrey and Banks):** a due-south track along **93.79°E**, ending at the "IG Hotspot" near **34.23°S** ([AirlineRatings](https://www.airlineratings.com/articles/new-search-areas-mh370-world-experts); [radiantphysics](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/)).

The 2025–26 search reportedly worked the Hotspot area without result ([radiantphysics](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2026/01/01/the-search-for-mh370-continues-into-2026/)).

Victor Iannello obtained ten Boeing end-of-flight simulations through the ATSB. In none of them did the aircraft fly wings-level after the autopilot disconnected. He recommended searching north of 25°S within ±25 NM of the arc ([radiantphysics](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2018/08/19/end-of-flight-simulations-of-mh370/)).

The CAPTION team (Marchand and Blelly) argues for a piloted glide ending 50–70 NM from the arc ([CAPTION](https://mh370-caption.net/)). GEOMAR's reverse drift modelling traced its particles to north of 30°S, with "very large" uncertainties ([radiantphysics](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/?p=297)).

Richard Godfrey's WSPR amateur-radio "passive radar" places the crash at **29.128°S, 99.934°E** ([mh370search.com](https://www.mh370search.com/?p=1419)). Iannello found its detector barely better than chance: a 52% true-positive rate against a 44% false-positive rate, when about 45% detections are expected from noise alone ([Metabunk](https://metabunk.org/goto/post?id=301066)). Godfrey's Liverpool collaborators have published no validation against known flights.

At the northern extreme, Iannello's blog promoted Ed Anderson's acoustic candidate in March 2026: **8.36°S, 107.92°E, about 42 miles off Java**. Iannello also cautioned that it is "impossible to definitively relate this acoustic event to MH370" ([radiantphysics](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2026/03/10/a-candidate-site-for-mh370-that-can-be-searched-quickly-and-soon/)).

WSPR is the closest precedent for what this report evaluates: reconstructing a track from incidental public sensors. It shows the failure mode to avoid. An unvalidated detector, run with prior knowledge of where the arcs lie, finds what it expects.

## Running the Doppler method in reverse fails over open ocean at night

### The satellite analysis already did reverse Doppler, with one sensor

The colsto technique, covered in the earlier report, fits the S-shaped Doppler curve of a passing propeller aircraft. It uses known aircraft tracks from ADS-B archives to find an unknown microphone ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UytYrhYLOKI)). Reversing it means fixing the sensor positions and solving for the aircraft. That is not new. Ferguson tracked the Doppler-shifted blade rate of a turboprop with sensors above and below the sea surface ([JASA 94(6), 1993, via Sonicfield](https://labs.sonicfield.org/library/doppler-effect-for-sound-emitted-by-a-moving-airborne-source-and-received-by-aco)). Dense seismic arrays now routinely catalogue overflights. Meng and Ben-Zion catalogued 1,816 aircraft events from 2014 on a nodal array near Anza, California ([JGR 2018](https://doi.org/10.1029/2017JB015240)). An Alaskan 500-sample-per-second line of 303 nodes classified about 1,200 passages by engine type ([UAF](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/news/seismic-data-can-identify-aircraft-type)).

MH370 has already been tracked by reverse Doppler of a kind. The Inmarsat BFO analysis inverted frequency offsets at **one known, moving sensor** to recover speed and heading, with BTO supplying range ([Ashton et al.](https://doi.org/10.1017/S037346331400068X)). The acoustic version reverses every one of those strengths:

| | Inmarsat BFO (done) | Ground acoustic or seismic (proposed) |
|---|---|---|
| Doppler size | ~10⁻⁶ fractional; needs precise oscillator modelling | Factor of ~3 swing at Mach 0.82; trivially measurable |
| Sensors | 1 satellite, always in view | Many fixed sensors, each must lie within ~40 km of the track |
| Sampling | 7 handshakes over 6 hours | Continuous, but only for the ~5 minutes the aircraft crosses each footprint |
| Range information | BTO rings | Closest-approach geometry only, with left/right ambiguity |

The Doppler-size and footprint figures above are my derivations from standard acoustics, set out in the next subsection. The point is that frequency precision is not the obstacle. Detection range and sensor density are.

### Three physical walls block a cruising 777

**Distance.** At about FL350, the closest possible slant range is about 10.7 km. That costs roughly 81 dB of spherical spreading, compared with 50–60 dB for a propeller aircraft at a few hundred metres. Atmospheric absorption over that path strips most energy above ~1 kHz, leaving only low-frequency jet and buzz-saw content. These are my calculations, using standard absorption behaviour.

The measurement literature agrees in outline. NASA characterises en-route jet noise as having "relatively low noise levels, lack of high frequency spectral content, and long durations" ([NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19900015539/downloads/19900015539.pdf)). The earlier research found that colsto clones missed almost all jets even at short range ([doppler-locate](https://github.com/digitalis-p/doppler-locate)).

**Refraction.** Air at FL350 is about −54 °C, and the tropical surface is about +28 °C. Under Snell's law, only rays that leave the aircraft at least about 31° below horizontal reach the ground. That limits direct sound to a corridor roughly **25–40 km either side of the track**, with a shadow zone beyond. Again these are my calculations, and upper-level winds can shift the corridor.

The best modern evidence is NORSAR's 2026 fibre-optic array. It saw "coherent lower-frequency arrivals ... even at cruising altitudes" and tracked their direction across the array. It could not resolve jet Doppler harmonics, because they exceeded its 62.5 Hz Nyquist limit ([NORFOX abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/GC14-FibreOptic/GC14-FibreOptic-42.html)). No published study has fitted a cruise-altitude jet Doppler curve from a single sparse station.

**Medium.** Hydrophones are worse still. Sound crosses from air into water only within about 12.8° of vertical. For an aircraft at 10.7 km, that limits exposure to roughly a 2.4 km radius, or about 20 seconds of flight (my calculation). Urick's measured P-3 overflight signature lasted "but a few seconds" ([Acoustics Today/Ferguson](https://acousticstoday.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/International-Student-Challenge-Problem-in-Acoustic-Signal-Processing-2019-Brian-G.-Ferguson-1.pdf)). The IMS hydrophones and IMOS moorings were hundreds to more than a thousand kilometres from any plausible cruise track.

Infrasound fares no better. The CTBTO's own review says commercial aircraft in normal flight are usually detected only within about 100 km of a station. It found "no detection or trace of signals related to the missing flight" within 5,000 km of the initial search area ([CTBTO IDC brief](https://www.ctbto.org/sites/default/files/Documents/IDC_infrasound_MalaysianAirlines_MH370.pdf)).

### The final moments carry no engine tone, and the sky held no audience

The question of tracking MH370's "final moments" runs into a physical fact. The ATSB's reading of the 00:19 UTC log-on is a restart on APU power **after fuel exhaustion**, followed by a steep descent ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf)). With both engines out, the stable turbofan tones that a Doppler fit needs were gone. What remained was aerodynamic noise and then the impact.

Only the impact plausibly radiated enough energy to be heard far away, and only through water. That makes the final minutes a question of hydroacoustic timing and bearing, not Doppler tracking.

Citizen recordings do not rescue the method. The radar-tracked overflight of the peninsula and the Strait took place at about **01:20–02:22 local time**. Everything after 18:22 UTC was over open ocean, at night. No yacht or ship recording has surfaced, and no public microphone was within reach. Kate Tee's Andaman Sea report was a visual sighting from a yacht, not a recording ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/oceania/mh370-search-did-sailor-see-plane-on-fire-1.1343029)).

The noise reports from the peninsula conflict with the radar track. Villagers in Marang heard a jet-like noise at about 01:20 local, and Kelantan witnesses reported low-flying aircraft heading north ([The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/nation/2014/03/12/cops-get-reports-on-lowflying-aircraft-and-loud-noise)). The radar shows a westbound track.

## Three hydroacoustic candidates point to three different places

### The events must not be conflated

Two of the three candidates were recorded at the CTBTO station HA01 off Cape Leeuwin, which some papers call H01W. The third is an independent claim. They differ in time, bearing and implied location. Every time quoted below is an arrival time at a sensor, unless it is marked as an origin time.

**The LANL/Kadri candidate is the official 2014 lead, found again.** Los Alamos flagged "an interesting arrival" at HA01 at about 00:52 UTC in July 2014. It was compromised by an overlapping dispersive Antarctic ice event at azimuth 190.5° ([ATSB App. J](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf); [OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1136454)).

Kadri's 2024 *Scientific Reports* paper analysed ten historical crashes, with signals detected at **2,211–4,695 km**. He reasoned that a crash near the 7th arc "should have been detected at least at H01W". He found exactly one signal of interest, this one, which was not confirmed at Diego Garcia because of airgun noise and a bathymetric rise ([Kadri 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60529-1)). His September 2025 CTBTO poster repeats that "plausible signals" exist and calls for calibration ([CTBTO SnT2025](https://conferences.ctbto.org/event/30/contributions/5606/)). In his own words: "If we run the experiments and we record nothing at the two stations, we cannot rely on the available hydrophone data" ([Changing Times](https://changingtimes.media/2024/06/22/mh370-experimental-ocean-blasts-could-bring-investigators-closer-to-the-truth/)). This research found no evidence that any calibration shot has been fired.

**The Curtin event's times are arrivals, not an origin.** The "~01:32 UTC" label belongs to the arrival onset at Scott Reef. The 01:3x times at Cape Leeuwin and the Perth Canyon reflect later arrivals at stations farther away. Curtin itself said the event was compatible with the timing of the last handshake but **incompatible with the satellite range** ([ATSB App. H](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf)).

The geometry explains both halves of that statement. By my calculation, the Carlsberg Ridge fix lies on a bearing of about 301.7° from HA01, matching the measured bearing almost exactly, at about 6,200 km. At a SOFAR-channel sound speed of 1.48 km/s, that implies an **origin around 00:24–00:25 UTC**, within minutes of the 00:19 log-on. Anderson's public seismic picks support an origin in that window: Minicoy, India at 00:28:40 UTC and the Maldives at 00:30:00 UTC ([370Location](https://370location.org/2017/09/mh370-locating-the-curtin-event-origin-with-seismometers)). Yet the ridge lies more than 4,000 km from the 7th arc. The match in timing is a coincidence of origin time, not of place.

**Anderson's Java event is internally consistent but physically awkward.** Its claimed origin of 01:15:18 UTC and its 01:59:20 UTC arrival at Diego Garcia fit a straight SOFAR path. By my calculation the path is about 3,900 km, taking about 44 minutes. That consistency suggests a real event near that point. It says nothing about the cause.

The event comes about **56 minutes after the final log-on**, so an MH370 explanation needs something like a floating wreck section sinking or imploding long after impact. A commenter on Iannello's blog argues the energy is orders of magnitude too large for that, and suggests a meteorite ([radiantphysics](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2026/03/10/a-candidate-site-for-mh370-that-can-be-searched-quickly-and-soon/)). The French hydrophone report and the Exmouth towed-array data that Anderson cites are not public.

### Reconciling where the notes disagree

The research notes contain several conflicts. The most consequential is a timing check that the notes did not perform.

The acoustic notes reason that a ~33-minute delay to a 00:52–00:54 arrival implies a source about 2,700–3,000 km away, "broadly consistent" with the mid-to-northern arc. Intersecting the measured bearing with the arc breaks that consistency. I approximated the 7th arc as a circle fitted through four published arc-adjacent points (scatter about ±75 km) and placed HA01 at about 34.9°S, 114.1°E. On those assumptions, a 306° ray from HA01 meets the arc only about **1,550–1,600 km out, near 26°S, 101°E**. An impact there at 00:20–00:23 UTC would reach HA01 at about **00:38–00:41 UTC, roughly 15 minutes earlier than the observed arrival**.

The observed 00:52–00:54 arrival on that bearing instead places a straight-line SOFAR source near **17°S, 92°E, more than 1,000 km beyond the arc**. Either the propagation is slower or more complex than straight-line SOFAR, or the arrival was picked late on an emergent, ice-contaminated signal, or the arrival is not from an impact on the arc. Kadri's acoustic-gravity-wave modelling and bathymetric blocking can change effective paths, so this check does not disprove the candidate. It is, however, the first test any replication should run.

The ~26°S crossing also lies inside the 25°S–36°S band that Godfrey says Ocean Infinity covered in 2018 ([mh370search.com](https://www.mh370search.com/2026/03/15/where-is-mh370/)).

One pattern deserves a warning. Several unrelated items cluster at about **01:15 UTC**:

- the Maldives sighting;
- Anderson's Java origin, at 01:15:18;
- Kadri's earlier source time for the Curtin event, 01:11–01:16;
- the unanswered handshake, at 01:15:56.

The handshake time was set by the ground station's schedule, not by anything happening to the aircraft. The Maldives and Java locations lie about 4,000 km apart by my calculation. At most one of these items can concern MH370. Shared timestamps are a known engine of confirmation bias in this case.

The case for keeping avenue 4 alive at all is narrow. A detection near Penang would refine the speed and timing that seed the satellite path models at the final major turn. It would not draw a new box on the seabed. Researchers have proposed acoustic, seismic and infrasound analyses of the impact for 12 years. This research found no published proposal to detect MH370's overflight in seismic data. That silence is weak evidence that analysts judged it implausible.

The catalogue shows an asymmetry. Anyone can reproduce the geometry: the arcs, bearings, travel times and candidate predictions. Anyone can also pull free seismic data from the two island stations closest to the southern route. Nobody outside an institution with a CTBTO contract can see the hydrophone waveforms on which two of the three candidates rest, and even those researchers cannot publish them.

## A validation-first plan with hard stop gates

The plan inverts the usual order. It proves each detector on events with known answers before it looks at 8 March 2014, and it commits in advance to stopping if a gate fails. That discipline is exactly what the WSPR dispute lacked.

| Stage | Goal | Method and data | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Pre-register the geometry | Make the predictions falsifiable | Compute the 7th arc from the Inmarsat log and BTO model rather than a fitted circle. For each candidate, predict arrival times and bearings at HA01, HA08, II.COCO, AU.XMIS, IN.MNC, GE.KAAM and Indonesian stations, with bathymetric blockage from GEBCO and GA grids. Publish the predictions before opening any waveform. | Re-run the 306°/00:52 timing check. If the ~15-minute discrepancy survives exact geometry, record it as a strike against that candidate. |
| 1. Validate impact detection on known events | Learn what public stations can hear | Measure T-phase detection at II.COCO, AU.XMIS and the Maldives/India stations for catalogued southern Indian Ocean earthquakes of small magnitude and for known seismic airgun surveys. Where public, also use the historical crashes in Kadri's set. | Report detection probability against range and magnitude. If an event of MH370-impact energy at 2,000–4,000 km is never seen, public seismic data cannot test the candidates. Stop avenue 2. |
| 2. Blind scan of 7–8 March 2014 | Test the three candidates without cherry-picking | Scan 23:30–02:30 UTC at all public stations with the Stage 1 detector, plus at least 20 control nights. Score Anderson's predicted arrivals (e.g. HA01 at about 01:49 UTC by my calculation) and the 306° candidate. Request IMOS PAPCA disks and check the duty cycle against each predicted arrival. | Accept only detections that beat the control-night false-alarm rate at multiple stations with consistent moveout. |
| 3. Validate overflight detection on modern traffic | Decide whether avenues 4 and 5 deserve any effort | Use ADS-B (adsb.lol, OpenSky) to list B777, A330 and A350 overflights at FL330–390 within 0–50 km of public 100+ sps stations (TM.SKLT, IN.PBA, Singapore). Stack spectrograms on predicted closest-approach times and fit a Doppler model with a stratified atmosphere. Benchmark against the Meng and Ben-Zion catalogue. | **If detection probability at 30–40 km lateral offset is near zero, abandon all overflight avenues.** That outcome is likely. |
| 4. Restricted-data requests | Reach the decisive records | Ask MetMalaysia for KUM, IPM and KTM waveforms for 17:20–18:30 UTC, but only if Stage 3 passes. Seek an institutional vDEC application for HA01/HA08 from 23:30 to 02:30 UTC, naming up to three analysts, with publication limited to derived results. | Proceed only with a partner organisation able to sign the CTBTO contract. |
| 5. Field calibration | Settle the 00:52 candidate | Propose airgun or explosive calibration shots of MH370-impact energy along the 7th arc, including near 26°S where the 306° bearing crosses it, during Ocean Infinity's November 2026 – April 2027 window. Record at HA01 and HA08. | Kadri's own rule: a much stronger signal at both stations weakens the candidate; nothing recorded at either station means the archive cannot help. |

Stages 0–3 need only public data, a laptop and a few weeks of an experienced analyst's time. They can reach a defensible negative result on their own. That would itself be worth publishing, because it would close the overflight question and narrow the hydroacoustic debate to one testable candidate. Stages 4 and 5 depend on institutions, and on a ship that is already contracted to return to the arc this season.

## Conclusion

Reversing the Doppler geolocation trick is not new physics, and it does not apply to MH370. Its failure is structural. The sensors were in the wrong places, the aircraft was too high, and the engines were probably silent at the end. Investigators already ran the one reverse-Doppler inversion the evidence allowed, with a single satellite, and it produced the arc everyone has been searching since. The productive reframing is to stop asking whether sound can track the aircraft and ask whether sound can time the impact. That question has a real, if weak, evidence base. It also has a clear, inexpensive experiment, calibration shots, that has gone unrun for more than two years since Kadri proposed it.

The reconciliation also changes the picture of the hydroacoustic record. The three candidates are not converging evidence. One is a ridge earthquake whose origin happened to fall near 00:19 UTC. One is a single-station arrival whose timing sits awkwardly against its own bearing. The third is a well-recorded event 56 minutes too late, ~4,000 km from the Maldives sighting that shares its 01:15 UTC timestamp. Each can be tested, and two of the three can be partly tested with free data. That makes a falsification campaign the most valuable contribution independent researchers can make while Ocean Infinity's last 7,429 km² waits for calm seas. It would replace a decade of suggestive coincidences with stated false-alarm rates.
