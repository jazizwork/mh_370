# Open reanalysis, not WSPR, can narrow MH370

The best way to narrow MH370's end point with free public data does not need a new sensor. It is an open, reproducible reanalysis of the evidence that already exists. Rebuild the probability of where the aircraft crossed the 7th arc from the public Inmarsat log, using open path solvers and ERA5 winds. Combine that with a joint debris-drift inversion that uses the real flaperon's measured leeway. Then discount every patch of seabed that sonar has already cleared. Two questions carry most of the value for deciding where to search next. The first is whether the aircraft glided after its fuel ran out, which makes the search corridor either about ±25 NM or ±70–140 NM wide. The second is whether the 25–30°S stretch of the arc holds up under a drift model with realistic leeway; several drift studies favour it, but it has never been searched to full width.

Open hydroacoustic and seismic data rank third. They cannot draw a new search box, but they can cheaply confirm or kill named candidates. Open seismometers already show that the 01:15 UTC "Java event" is real but is a natural earthquake of about magnitude 4.1, roughly 20 times the aircraft's kinetic energy. Contrails and other radio or atmospheric signals fail on physics before the data are even examined.

WSPR ranks last, and its method is unsound. It needs a 777 to shift a spot's reported SNR by about 1 dB, but the bistatic radar equation allows at most about 0.03 dB. Its 0.75-standard-deviation anomaly rule fires about 45% of the time by chance, and its validation tests were not blind. Its end point has moved about 4° of latitude, and on the night no short-path WSPR link crossed the southern arc after 23:06 UTC. A pre-registered blind test using wspr.live and adsb.lol could settle the claim within weeks.

As of 5 November 2026, Ocean Infinity's no-find-no-fee contract runs to 30 June 2027, with 7,428.54 km² of its 15,000 km² area still unsearched and no restart announced. Public data can realistically re-rank that area and the regions beyond it. It cannot pinpoint the wreck.

## Twelve years of sonar leave 7,429 km² and an untested north

### The southern band near the arc is largely cleared

Three seabed campaigns have now covered most of the ground that the satellite data and the southern drift studies favour.

- **ATSB, 2014–2017.** The sonar search covered **about 120,000 km² between roughly 32.8°S and 39.5°S**, cost about A$198 million and was suspended on 17 January 2017 ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf); [ATSB overview](https://www.atsb.gov.au/mh370-search-overview)).
- **Ocean Infinity, 2018.** It searched more than 112,000 km² north of the ATSB zone ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_370)). Richard Godfrey's tally puts Ocean Infinity's coverage since 2018 at about 140,000 km² between 36°S and 25°S ([mh370search.com, 15 Mar 2026](https://www.mh370search.com/2026/03/15/where-is-mh370/)).
- **Ocean Infinity, 2025–2026.** Malaysia signed a new no-find-no-fee deal on 25 March 2025 for a 15,000 km² area, with a **US$70 million fee payable only on success**. Phase 1 ran 25–28 March 2025. Phase 2, with the vessel Armada 86 05, ran 31 December 2025 to 23 January 2026. Together they took **28 operational days to survey about 7,571 km² and found nothing**. The official update gives no coordinates ([MOT update to families](https://www.mot.gov.my/en/Kenyataan%20Media/Year%202026/MH370%20Search%20Operation%202025-2026%20-%20Update%20to%20Families.pdf)).

Ocean Infinity's chief executive summed it up: "we can say with confidence that it isn't where we looked" ([Ocean Infinity](https://oceaninfinity.com/news/conclusion-of-the-search-for-malaysian-airlines-flight-mh370/)).

The 2025–26 work centred on the Independent Group's hotspot near **34.23°S 93.78°E**. In March 2025 the vessel covered the UGIB Last Estimated Position and the High Priority Search Zone. In January 2026 Armada 86 05 worked outward, southeast of the arc, near 35.1–35.2°S, 93.4–93.7°E ([Radiant Physics, 1 Jan 2026](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2026/01/01/the-search-for-mh370-continues-into-2026/)). That work included the CAPTION group's zone centred at 35.7°S 93°E ([CAPTION vessel tracking](https://www.mh370-caption.net/index.php/armada-tracking/)).

Bobby Ulich, a UGIB co-author, wrote in March 2026 that latitudes from about 33°S to 39°S "have been thoroughly searched without success", with **95–98% of the area within ±25 NM of the arc cleared** ([Radiant Physics comments, 10 Mar 2026](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2026/03/10/a-candidate-site-for-mh370-that-can-be-searched-quickly-and-soon/)). That is a blog comment, not an official figure, but no source contradicts it. It shapes everything that follows. The open question is no longer just where along the arc MH370 ended. It is how far off the arc, and how far north.

### The contract is live, but nobody has announced a restart

On 29 June 2026 Malaysia's Cabinet extended the contract to **30 June 2027, on unchanged terms, to cover the remaining 7,428.54 km²** ([FMT](https://www.freemalaysiatoday.com/category/nation/2026/06/29/mh370-search-deal-with-ocean-infinity-extended-for-another-year); [The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/nation/2026/06/29/govt-extends-deal-with-ocean-infinity-to-search-for-mh370-wreckage)).

Reports read the November–April window in opposite ways. The Star says redeployment "can only be conducted between November 2026 and April 2027". The Los Angeles Times says the vessels are expected to return to the MH370 mission in that calm-sea window ([LA Times](https://www.latimes.com/world-nation/story/2026-06-29/malaysia-extends-search-for-mh370-for-another-year-keeping-families-hopes-alive)). The Times of India reports instead that Ocean Infinity's commercial commitments will require the search's primary assets to be "temporarily redeployed to another location between November 2026 and April 2027" ([Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/world/rest-of-world/closure-for-next-of-kin-malaysia-extends-mh370-search-by-one-more-year/articleshow/132068764.cms)).

This research found no Ministry of Transport or Ocean Infinity statement of a restart date or vessel departure as of 5 November 2026. On 3 November, community vessel-watchers were still waiting to see whether Armada 86 05 would head south ([YouTube, 3 Nov 2026](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/u6TDnKib2KM)). Neither the 15,000 km² search polygon nor the 7,571 km² actually swept has ever been published.

The WSPR circle is often misreported, so its status needs care. In January 2026 the search vessels were near 35°S 93°E. On 3 January Ed Anderson wrote that "the broader 'WSPR' area might warrant a search", which implies it had not been searched ([Radiant Physics, 1 Jan 2026](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2026/01/01/the-search-for-mh370-continues-into-2026/)).

The authors also contradict each other about earlier coverage. Their February 2024 paper reports "no overlap" with earlier searches, "a gap of 19.9 km". A December 2023 comment says "less than 50% of the area was covered" ([Godfrey, Coetzee & Maskell, Feb 2024](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/vzftcvfx01lhbt3xfgyu5/How-Does-WSPR-Detect-Aircraft-over-Long-Distances-15FEB2024.pdf?rlkey=p8dcu8q3ww741joa922bdikng&dl=0); [mh370search.com, Dec 2023](https://www.mh370search.com/2023/12/22/mh370-search-area/)).

Wikipedia's WSPR article says two Ocean Infinity searches in 2025 and 2026 "in the suggested region" failed. Its citation does not mention WSPR, and the region it means is the 2021 point, not the current one ([Wikipedia: WSPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WSPR_(Amateur_radio_software)); [Ocean Infinity](https://oceaninfinity.com/news/conclusion-of-the-search-for-malaysian-airlines-flight-mh370/)).

The current WSPR location is therefore **untested at sea, not refuted at sea**. Only its first version, 33.2°S 95.3°E, has been tested. In 2022 Geoscience Australia re-reviewed 4,900 km² of the 2014–17 sonar there. It found 11 previously unanalysed contacts, none of them aircraft, and judged a debris field "highly unlikely" ([ATSB/GA statement via Mirage News](https://www.miragenews.com/mh370-data-review-statement-from-australian-768527/)).

## Reanalysis and drift outrank every new-signal idea

The ranking scores each avenue on two things.

- **Expected value:** the chance that its best realistic result would change where the next AUV dives, weighted by how far it would move the search.
- **Feasibility:** whether it can be done with free data, with no permits, applications or contracts.

The arcs from the Inmarsat data are not in doubt. Each avenue is judged on whether it can resolve the assumptions the arcs leave open.

| Rank | Avenue | Best realistic result | Expected value for narrowing | Feasibility with free data | Call |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Satellite BTO/BFO reanalysis (path ensemble plus end-of-flight BFO) | Arc-crossing likelihood for each autopilot mode, and a glide/no-glide verdict that sets corridor width | **Moderate**, and the highest available | **High**: public log, open solver, free ERA5; weeks of work | Core task |
| 2 | Debris drift and barnacles | A north (25–30°S) versus south (34–36°S) verdict that holds across leeway and ocean models | **Moderate**; the only independent latitude evidence | **High** for drift; **low** for barnacles (the oldest shells are withheld) | Core task |
| 3 | Open hydroacoustic and seismic data | Kill or confirm the Java, 306°, 247° and Kimberley candidates; at best a two-station fix | **Low** for a new location; **high** falsification value per hour spent | **High**: IMOS files and FDSN waveforms for the night verified | Quick parallel sprint |
| 4 | Satellite imagery and contrails | A persistent contrail tying down one track segment | **Very low**: ERA5 shows contrails would not persist; no MODIS or VIIRS pass over the arc 19:25–03:00 UTC | **Medium**: ERA5 needs no login; imagery needs free accounts, some unverified | ERA5 screen only, with a kill rule |
| 5 | WSPR "passive radar" | For location: nothing unless physics is wrong by 30–100 dB. For the community: settles the 29°S claim | **Near zero** for location | **High**: wspr.live and adsb.lol are free | Run only as a blind test |
| 6 | Other radio and atmospheric signals | MWA, GNSS TEC, lightning and JORN give nothing; ERA5 is a path-model input; ADS-B and AIS give witness context | **Negligible**, except ERA5 as an input | Mixed: JORN classified, WWLLN paid, OpenSky by application | Drop, except ERA5 |

### The arcs are settled; the path between them is not

The public 47-page log was released on 27 May 2014. Copies converted to CSV are on GitHub, and they hold each handshake's burst timing offset (BTO) and burst frequency offset (BFO) ([sladen/inmarsat-9m-mro](https://github.com/sladen/inmarsat-9m-mro); [BigDataMalaysia](https://github.com/BigDataMalaysia/MH370_Inmarsat)).

The calibration is well documented. The **BTO bias is −495,679 μs**, the mean of 17 measurements taken before takeoff, and the **fixed BFO bias is 150 Hz** ([Ashton et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S037346331400068X)). From 20 earlier flights, the BFO error has σ = 4.3 Hz, and all 2,501 valid in-flight errors fall between −28 and +18 Hz ([Holland 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.02432)).

Those numbers fix the arcs. They do not fix the path between them. UGIB modelled about 2,300 candidate paths. On satellite data alone the result peaked near 34.3°S, with other peaks to the north and south, and fuel and drift arguments broke the tie ([Radiant Physics, Mar 2020](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2020/03)). Inmarsat's own example route ended at 34.7°S 93.0°E, and its authors stressed "significant uncertainty in the final location" ([Ashton et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S037346331400068X)).

Three open variables decide the answer.

**1. Did the aircraft glide?** This now carries the most decision value.

- Holland's analysis of the last two BFOs bounds the descent rate at **3,900–14,800 ft/min at 00:19:29 and 14,800–25,300 ft/min eight seconds later**. That is a downward acceleration of about 0.68 g, which points to a steep, unpiloted dive close to the arc ([Holland 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.02432)). The ATSB's figures are similar: 2,900–15,200 ft/min, then 13,800–25,300 ft/min ([ATSB final report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773565/operational-search-for-mh370_final_3oct2017.pdf)).
- Vincent Lyne's peer-reviewed 2024 paper rejects Holland's model and argues for a "controlled eastward descent" ([Lyne 2024](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3B8A244FB86B058D36A406BE6EB17345/S0373463324000262a.pdf/final-two-mh370-communications-suggest-controlled-eastward-descent.pdf)).
- Either answer changes the next search, because the ±25 NM southern corridor is largely cleared. A confirmed dive pushes probability north and into the data holidays. A confirmed glide sends the next search into the 22,000–166,000 km² off-arc zones.

**2. Which navigation and speed mode did the aircraft fly after the final major turn?**

- Each combination gives a different arc latitude. The options include great-circle LNAV, constant true or magnetic track, and heading hold, at Mach or long-range-cruise speed.
- The open MH370Solver code fits paths by minimising BTO/BFO residuals ([MH370Solver](https://github.com/o-nesterov/MH370Solver)).
  - It covers true and magnetic track and heading, IAS and Mach speed modes, and level or descending flight.
  - It takes GDAS1 or ERA5 winds.
- This research found no published open comparison of all modes with residual statistics.

**3. When was the final major turn?** The log holds 51 BFOs from the 18:39:55–18:40:56 call, all between 86 and 90 Hz. That narrow spread limits how much the aircraft could have been turning or climbing in that minute ([Ashton et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S037346331400068X)).

The data that would sharpen all three are not public:

- the unredacted log fields (carrier-to-noise ratio, received power and bit-error rate);
- the BFOs from 9M-MRO's 20 earlier flights;
- the logs of other aircraft that Inmarsat used for validation ([Ashton et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S037346331400068X)).

The public log is still enough to produce something that does not yet exist: a reproducible likelihood for each mode, tested for sensitivity to winds and published with its code.

### Drift splits on leeway, and the northern answer is untested

Published inverse-drift studies fall into two families, and the split follows one modelling choice: how they treat leeway.

The southern family uses the real flaperon's leeway. CSIRO measured it in 2017 on a cut-down 777 flaperon, which drifted **about 20° left of the wind and faster than the replicas** ([CSIRO](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/All/Articles/2017/April/mh370)). These studies land at 34–36°S:

- CSIRO: **35.6°S 92.8°E** ([CSIRO, Aug 2017](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/all/articles/2017/august/satellite-images-add-to-weight-of-evidence-locating-missing-mh370)).
- Godfrey, 2021: **34.13°S ±1.06°** ([AirlineRatings](https://www.airlineratings.com/articles/mh370-new-credible-evidence-location)).
- Ulich and Iannello, 2023: **34.0°S**, with a plausible range of 30–36°S, from 2 × 86,400 BRAN2015 drift trials and 17 debris reports ([Radiant Physics, Jun 2023](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2023/06)).

The northern family uses generic particles, drifter Markov chains or early, warm-biased barnacle temperatures:

- GEOMAR: north of about 30°S ([GEOMAR](https://www.geomar.de/en/news/article/where-is-mh370)).
- Nesterov: 25.5–30.5°S ([Nesterov 2018](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/14/387/2018/)).
- Miron et al.: about 25°S ([Miron et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.5092132)).

The Italian CMCC superensemble spans 28–35°S ([Jansen et al. 2016](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/16/1623/2016/)).

The split creates an uncomfortable tension. The best-calibrated drift answer points to ground that has already been searched. Either the wreck lies in a gap or off the arc in the south, or the drift models are wrong and the north deserves a full-width search.

A joint test could decide between them, and nobody has run one. This research found no study that does any of the following:

- formalise the absence of debris on the Western Australian coast as a likelihood term (CSIRO used it only qualitatively; [CSIRO](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/All/Articles/2017/April/mh370));
- run several ocean reanalyses jointly;
- combine debris find-time likelihoods with the barnacle isotope record.

All the inputs are free:

- [NOAA's Global Drifter Program](https://www.aoml.noaa.gov/phod/gdp/), with drogue-loss flags;
- [Copernicus Marine](https://marine.copernicus.eu/) GLORYS12 reanalysis and altimetry;
- [HYCOM](https://www.hycom.org/);
- OSCAR currents;
- ERA5 winds;
- the [OpenDrift](https://opendrift.github.io/) and [Parcels](https://oceanparcels.org/) frameworks.

Barnacles add less than the headlines suggest. In 2023 Al-Qattan, Herbert and colleagues published the first lab-calibrated temperature equation for *Lepas anatifera*. They showed that earlier calibrations ran several degrees warm, and they reconstructed a more southerly flaperon path, but **only for its last five months** ([Al-Qattan et al. 2023](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023AV000915)).

They assumed negligible windage, which conflicts with CSIRO's field trials. The decisive shells, the largest and oldest, which probably settled near the crash site, are held by the French judiciary. The paper says "only partial drift reconstructions are possible until the largest, oldest barnacles are released for study by the French government" ([Al-Qattan et al. 2023](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023AV000915)).

Their model data are open on Mendeley ([Herbert 2023](https://doi.org/10.17632/nr4vt4h8gt.4)). With that, the feasible task is to re-run their analysis with CSIRO-style leeway as a sensitivity test. It will not produce a new latitude.

### Open hydrophones can kill candidates but rarely create them

The open data here are better than commonly described, and three common descriptions need correcting.

- **The IMOS recordings exist and are downloadable.** The Perth Canyon (PAPCA), Kimberley (PAKIM) and Portland recorders all have raw files for 8 March 2014 in a public S3 bucket. Each records **300 s of every 15 minutes at 6 kHz**. A reader and per-deployment calibration curves are published alongside ([AODN acoustic bucket](https://imos-data.s3-ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&prefix=IMOS/ANMN/Acoustic/&delimiter=/); [PAPCA 8 Mar 2014](https://imos-data.s3-ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&prefix=IMOS/ANMN/Acoustic/PAPCA/3376/20140308/)).
- **The 2014 "Scott Reef" recorder is very probably the Kimberley deployment.** Curtin University's 2014 analysis used a "Scott Reef" recorder. The AODN Kimberley deployment is the only IMOS recorder in north-west Australia for that period. Its 01:30:00–01:35:08 file contains a strong transient at about 01:32:56 UTC, close to the 01:32:49 arrival reported in 2014 ([PAKIM file](https://imos-data.s3-ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/IMOS/ANMN/Acoustic/PAKIM/3250/20140308/raw/531A7289.DAT)).
- **The Los Alamos bearing was 246.9°, not 306°.** Los Alamos flagged a candidate at Cape Leeuwin's CTBTO station HA01 at a bearing of **246.9°**. The 306.18° arrival at 00:54:30 is Kadri's ([Stead 2014](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1136454); [Kadri 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60529-1.pdf)).

Public FDSN services return full waveforms for many island and coastal stations near the arc ([FDSN station inventory](https://service.iris.edu/fdsnws/station/1/query?minlat=-50&maxlat=10&minlon=60&maxlon=130&starttime=2014-03-08T00:00:00&endtime=2014-03-08T02:00:00&level=station&format=text); [GEOFON](https://geofon.gfz.de/fdsnws/dataselect/1/)):

- Cocos, Christmas Island, Diego Garcia, Amsterdam Island and Rodrigues;
- the 13-element Pilbara array and Busselton on the Western Australian coast;
- nine Indonesian GEOFON stations.

The CTBTO hydrophone and infrasound channels are listed in the station metadata but return no data.

The table shows why this avenue earns third place and no higher.

**The Java event is real but far too energetic.** Open seismometers record it at Cisarua (GE.CISI, 90 km away) at 371 times background. At Christmas Island (AU.XMIS) it reached 28 times background. A catalogued M4.1 earthquake south of Java at 23:52, at a similar distance, gave 16.4 times ([USGS catalogue](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query?format=csv&starttime=2014-03-07T18:00:00&endtime=2014-03-08T03:00:00&minlatitude=-60&maxlatitude=15&minlongitude=40&maxlongitude=140&minmagnitude=2); [GEOFON](https://geofon.gfz.de/fdsnws/dataselect/1/)).

A first, single-band amplitude scaling put the event near M4.3 (about 180 GJ). The project's later seven-station analysis refines this to **M≈4.1 ± 0.2**, about 9×10¹⁰ J. That is roughly **20 times** the 4 GJ of kinetic energy Kadri estimates for a 200 t aircraft hitting the water at 200 m/s ([Kadri 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60529-1.pdf)). It located the event at 8.12–8.30°S, origin 01:15:19–24 UTC, with earthquake-like S/P ratios (see `mh370-open/outputs/acoustic/README.md`).

The same open data raise a further possibility. The Kimberley transient could be the Java event's T-phase, arriving 45–65 s early through a partly crustal path. If so, the 2014 association of the Perth Canyon, HA01 and Scott Reef detections into one Carlsberg Ridge source mixed separate events. That needs testing, not assuming.

**Kadri's 306° candidate has a timing problem.** The bearing meets the arc 1,593 km from HA01, at 25.7°S 101.4°E. An impact there at 00:19–00:21 would reach HA01 at about 00:37–00:39, which is 15–17 minutes before the observed arrival. Kadri accepts that the aircraft would have had to stay airborne at least 10 minutes after the last handshake ([Kadri 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60529-1.pdf)). That conflicts with Holland's BFO-derived dive.

**One loose end could still become significant.** Perth Canyon shows a +6–8 dB burst at 00:53:11–00:53:25, coinciding with the predicted arrival from Kadri's arc crossing. If that burst proved impulsive and unique against control days, it would pair with the HA01 bearing to give a fix. Its narrowband character near 10–12 Hz points to a whale or a ship. Treat it as a coincidence to vet, not a lead.

**Sensitivity is limited.**

- Island seismometers are typically 20–40 dB less sensitive to T-phases than hydrophones, and they sample only 20–40 times a second ([OSTI 289887 via openpolar](https://openpolar.no/Record/ftosti:oai:osti.gov:289887)). They cannot see an impact equivalent to M2–3 more than 1,000 km away. They are good at auditing larger claimed events.
- Ambient noise at Perth Canyon is 95–101 dB re 1 µPa ([PAPCA file](https://imos-data.s3-ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/IMOS/ANMN/Acoustic/PAPCA/3376/20140308/raw/531A6929.DAT)). A 0.5 Pa arrival like the one Kadri expects at HA01 would register only if propagation to that shallower site were no more than about 10 dB worse. A non-detection there is weak evidence; a detection would be strong evidence.

The decisive HA01 and HA08 waveforms stay behind the CTBTO's vDEC contract ([CTBTO vDEC](https://www.ctbto.org/specials/vdec/)). Popular Mechanics published the French OHASISBIO report on 16 July 2026. It found no impact near the arc, and its raw data are available only on request ([Jeff Wise repost](https://www.jeffwise.net/?p=7076)).

### Contrails and stray signals fail on physics first

ERA5 reanalysis, which can be downloaded without a login, answers the contrail question before any image is opened. This research queried ARCO-ERA5 for the key times and places ([ARCO-ERA5 via pycontrails](https://py.contrails.org/notebooks/ARCO-ERA5.html)):

- **Pre-turn leg, Andaman Sea, 18:00–19:00 UTC.** The Schmidt–Appleman formation criterion was met in **0% of the region at FL340–FL360**, and the air was strongly ice-subsaturated. No contrail is expected on this leg.
- **IG and CSIRO end points, 00:00 UTC.** The formation criterion was met, but relative humidity over ice was only 13–77%. Any contrail would have vanished within minutes.
- **WSPR point, 00:00 UTC.** Contrails could not form at FL340–FL360.
- **Elsewhere.** Ice-supersaturated patches existed at about 38–40°S, and at 28–30°S near 105°E.

ERA5 is known to underestimate ice supersaturation ([Hildebrandt et al. 2026](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/26/6449/2026/)), so these results are indicative, not conclusive.

Imaging coverage is thin as well:

- No MODIS or VIIRS pass covered the 7th-arc zone between about 19:25 and 03:00 UTC. Terra imaged all three candidate end points only at about 03:00, some 2.7 hours after the end ([NASA CMR](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=MYD021KM&temporal=2014-03-07T15:00:00Z,2014-03-08T05:00:00Z&bounding_box=80,-42,108,12&page_size=200)).
- The 2014 geostationary imagers had 4–5 km infrared pixels and a 30–60 minute cadence. Meteosat-7 lacked the split-window channels used to detect contrails.
- Even modern 2 km GOES imagery sees only **about 46% of contrail segments** in ideal clear-ocean conditions ([Driver et al. 2025](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/18/1115/2025/)).

The two published MH370 contrail claims were never reproduced blind, and both fare poorly against the ERA5 humidity check. One is Vasquez's Meteosat-7 feature at 46°S, which he himself called "inconclusive" ([Weather Graphics](https://www.weathergraphics.com/malaysia/contrail.shtml)). The other is Ulich and Prostyakov's line at 39.6–39.9°S ([Metabunk](https://www.metabunk.org/threads/claim-mh370-contrails-have-been-identified.6068/)). Separately, Geoscience Australia's 2017 analysis of Pléiades satellite images from 23 March 2014 found 12 "probably man-made" floating objects, but it could not tie them to aircraft debris ([GA report](https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/media/5773373/mh370_satellite-imagery-geoscienceaust-report.pdf)).

The remaining signals are weaker still:

- **Murchison Widefield Array.** It has detected objects by FM reflection ([MWA, PASA 2020](https://www.cambridge.org:443/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BF1BFD69F15D72D65514E95868F21DBA/S1323358020000405a.pdf/lowfrequency_blind_survey_of_the_low_earth_orbit_environment_using_noncoherent_passive_radar_with_the_murchison_widefield_array.pdf)). But a FL350 aircraft's radio horizon from the ground is about 426 km, and every southern candidate point is 1,660–3,300 km away.
- **GNSS ionospheric data.** No MH370 study using it exists. The method detects tsunamis and earthquakes, which release vastly more energy than an aircraft impact ([Komjathy et al.](https://earth-planets-space.springeropen.com/counter/pdf/10.5047/eps.2012.08.003.pdf)).
- **Lightning.** WWLLN data are sold "at nominal cost" and carry no aircraft signature ([WWLLN](https://webflash.ess.washington.edu/archive.html)).
- **JORN.** Australia's over-the-horizon radar reportedly "does not operate on a 24-hour basis", and its records are classified ([Reuters via Yahoo](https://news.yahoo.com/australia-takes-over-southern-arc-search-missing-malaysian-052803864--sector.html)).

Three datasets keep a supporting role. ERA5 is the best atmospheric input to path models. The CAPTION group's FlightAware-based list of flights that crossed MH370's path (the closest at about 12 NM) and Global Fishing Watch's free AIS ship archive from 2012 identify potential witnesses. They do not locate the aircraft ([CAPTION](https://www.mh370-caption.net/index.php/possible-mh370-encounters-in-the-southern-indian-ocean/); [Global Fishing Watch](https://globalfishingwatch.org/platform-update/global-ais-vessel-presence-dataset/)).

## WSPR fails on physics, statistics and test design

### How the method works and where it stands

The WSPR/GDTAAA claim comes from Richard Godfrey with Dr Hannes Coetzee and, from 2023, Professor Simon Maskell of the University of Liverpool. Dr Robert Westphal first proposed the idea in 2020. The method treats the WSPR amateur radio network's database of decoded spots as a global passive radar.

- **Anomaly:** for each transmitter–receiver link, a spot whose SNR, frequency or drift is **more than 0.75 standard deviations from that link's mean over ±3 hours**.
- **Detection:** an anomaly whose link great circle passes a candidate aircraft position in the same 2-minute slot ([Godfrey, Coetzee & Maskell, Feb 2024](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/vzftcvfx01lhbt3xfgyu5/How-Does-WSPR-Detect-Aircraft-over-Long-Distances-15FEB2024.pdf?rlkey=p8dcu8q3ww741joa922bdikng&dl=0)).
- **Track:** built forward under a 536.2 kt speed cap, with turns snapped to waypoints ([mh370search.com, Dec 2023](https://www.mh370search.com/2023/12/22/mh370-search-area/)).

The claimed impact point has moved from **33.177°S 95.300°E** in December 2021 to **29.128°S 99.934°E ±30 km** in August 2023 ([mh370search.com, Dec 2021](https://www.mh370search.com/2021/12/31/mh370-flight-path/); [AirlineRatings](https://airlineratings.com/articles/two-new-reports-verify-mh370-location-technology)).

Every report is self-published.

- Liverpool announced a validation study on other 777 flights in March 2024 ([University of Liverpool](https://news.liverpool.ac.uk/2024/03/06/university-researchers-provide-statistical-expertise-to-help-locate-mh370/)), and it has not been published.
- In May 2026 Maskell still framed the question as open: "there are other people that are firmly of the opinion that it doesn't. So we're trying to figure out who's right" ([Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/future-technology/solving-mh370-mystery)). He is a co-author of the very reports under test, so the Liverpool study is not independent.
- Godfrey's own March 2026 analysis no longer advances a WSPR crash location ([mh370search.com, Mar 2026](https://www.mh370search.com/2026/03/15/where-is-mh370/)).
- The ATSB has said it "cannot offer an assessment" of the WSPR work ([ATSB](https://www.atsb.gov.au/news/2022/mh370-statement)).

This research's verdict is that the method's errors are serious enough to give its MH370 location no evidential weight. Some problems are physics arguments that rest on modelling assumptions. Most are checkable facts about the data and the procedure.

### The effect it needs is 30 to 100 dB larger than physics allows

WSPR stores no waveform. The reference decoder averages each 110.6-second transmission into a single spectrum. It reports SNR in whole decibels relative to its own noise estimate, and "drift" as a whole-number linear frequency change across the transmission ([wsprd.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/saitohirga/WSJT-X/master/lib/wsprd/wsprd.c); [wspr.live schema](https://wspr.live/)).

An aircraft could change those numbers only if the energy it scatters is comparable to the direct signal. The required size follows from the arithmetic:

- To shift the reported SNR by 1 dB, even with perfectly in-phase addition, the scattered power must be no more than about 18 dB below the direct signal.
- The standard bistatic radar equation, using an optimistic forward-scatter radar cross-section for a 777, gives the ratios below.

| Geometry (14 MHz, σ = 42.7 dBsm) | Scattered power relative to direct | Largest possible SNR change |
|---|---|---|
| 4,000 km link, aircraft mid-path | −88 dB | 0.0003 dB |
| 4,000 km link, aircraft 10 km from transmitter | −48 dB | 0.03 dB |
| 16,527 km HB9CZF→VK1CH, the MH370 case Iannello analysed | −100 dB | 0.0001 dB |
| 252 km W8AC→N8GA, the proponents' "short-distance" link, mid-path | −64 dB | 0.005 dB |
| **Needed to produce a 1 dB change** | **−18 dB** | **1 dB** |

These figures are this research's calculation, and they agree with published critiques. Victor Iannello's link budget for HB9CZF→VK1CH gives an S/N of **−81 dB, against about −30 dB needed** to decode. He checked his method against a real measurement of aircraft scatter and matched it within 4 dB ([Iannello 2021](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2021/12/19/wspr-cant-find-mh370/)). The radio amateur Nils Schiffhauer (DK8OK) has watched aircraft scatter on 250 kW broadcast carriers. He finds the effect on overall signal level is "almost always well below 0.3 dB", while shortwave levels swing 30 dB within seconds ([DK8OK via rtl-sdr.com](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/nils-critiques-the-mh370-wspr-aircraft-scatter-theory/)).

Purpose-built over-the-horizon radars do detect aircraft. JORN uses 560 kW and kilometre-long arrays ([Wikipedia: JORN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jindalee_Operational_Radar_Network)). It also separates the Doppler-shifted echo from the direct signal, which WSPR's stored fields cannot do.

The table rests on measurements made for this report on [wspr.live](https://wspr.live/).

- **Normal slot-to-slot variation.** On the same link, the standard deviation of SNR change between consecutive 2-minute slots was 3.5–4.7 dB. 40–55% of consecutive pairs differed by 3 dB or more. Drift was zero in 75–84% of spots.
- **The flagged link on the night.** HB9CZF→VK6ZRY crossed the 7th-arc box and logged ten spots between 20:56 and 23:06 UTC. SNR ranged from −22 to −26 dB, drift was −1 every time, and frequency stayed within a 5 Hz band. The single +3 dB excursion is a routine one-in-ten event.
- **Network density.** The 2014 network was thin, at about 281,000 spots on 7 March 2014 against 7.8 million on 7 March 2025. In the region it had three genuine transmitters, at Bangkok, Singapore and Perth.

Godfrey reports that Maskell found a 2022 report "logical and statistically correct" ([AirlineRatings](https://www.airlineratings.com/articles/critics-of-wsprnet-tracking-mh370-been-answered)). The quantity that matters is different: the effect size the claimed AUC implies.

- An AUC of 0.60 corresponds to a mean SNR shift of about 1.2 dB on a 3.3 dB per-spot spread.
- Physics allows at most about 0.03 dB, which corresponds to an AUC of about 0.503.
- A genuine AUC of 0.6 would therefore mean the bistatic radar equation is wrong by 30–100 dB.

The economical explanation is selection bias.

### The strongest case for WSPR still lacks one blind success

A fair verdict should state the best case for WSPR:

- HF scatter from aircraft is real.
- Over-the-horizon radar works.
- WSPRnet is a huge multi-frequency dataset.
- The proponents report above-chance ROC curves on several flights.
- A Singapore DSTA student project found "preliminary" support for SNR-anomaly detection, though it also stated: "We are unable to conclusively verify that data from WSPRnet can be used to detect and localise aircraft" ([mh370search.com, May 2025](https://www.mh370search.com/2024/05/05/new-search/comment-page-10/)).
- No measured HF radar cross-section of a 777 exists, so the physics critiques rest on modelled values. Even the most generous modelled value, however, leaves a gap of tens of decibels.
- The 29°S circle has never been searched.

Disputes about Godfrey's professional background, and the heated rhetoric on both sides, are irrelevant to whether the method works. None of the case for WSPR includes one blind, pre-registered success, and Joe Taylor, who created WSPR, reportedly does not believe the historical data "can provide any information useful for aircraft tracking" ([Iannello 2021](https://mh370.radiantphysics.com/2021/12/19/wspr-cant-find-mh370/)).

### A blind test could settle the claim within weeks

A decisive test needs only free data. WSPR spots come from wspr.live: SQL over HTTP, no sign-up, a limit of 20 requests a minute, and non-commercial use only ([wspr.live](https://wspr.live/)). Ground truth comes from adsb.lol: daily ADS-B archives from 2023, about 2 GB per day per receiver instance, under the ODbL licence ([adsb.lol](https://www.adsb.lol/docs/open-data/historical/); [globe_history_2024](https://github.com/adsblol/globe_history_2024)). OpenSky's historical database requires an application, so it is excluded.

The protocol below is this research's design.

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Pre-registration | Publish the hypotheses, fields, thresholds, windows, path model, crossing width and handling of missing spots, with a hash, before any labels exist. Short paths are primary; long paths count only for links of 15,000 km or more |
| Link selection | Every transmitter–receiver–band combination with at least 100 spots a day on 7, 10 and 14 MHz. Stratify by distance: under 500, 500–3,000, 3,000–10,000 and over 10,000 km. Both ends must be in good ADS-B coverage. Repeat on a sample thinned to 1/28 to mimic 2014 density |
| Exposure | Any aircraft at FL200 or above within D km of the path during the 110.6 s transmission, with D set to the proponents' preferred width. A physics variant counts only crossings within the first Fresnel zone, in ray segments near the endpoints and hop points |
| Controls | The same link and day within ±1 h with no aircraft nearby. ADS-B shifted by whole days (±1 to ±14) as an empirical null. Placebo links that share the receiver. A positive control: inject +1 dB into 10% of crossed slots and confirm the pipeline detects it |
| Blinding | Commit-reveal: the ADS-B analyst publishes a SHA-256 hash of the labels before seeing any scores. Alternatively, a decoy-track test: N real tracks mixed with N time-shifted or rotated decoys, which the method must tell apart |
| Primary endpoint | AUC of the SNR anomaly score for crossed versus uncrossed slots, stratified by link, one-sided α = 0.05. Secondary endpoints Holm-corrected; a Westfall–Young permutation over all scores and bands |
| Power | Crossings needed: 39–244 for a 1 dB effect (what the proponents' AUC implies); 155–975 for 0.5 dB; 0.4–2.4 million for 0.01 dB (what physics allows) |
| MH370 transfer test | Blinded track recovery on real long-haul segments at 2014 density. The method must beat a naive arc-based prior by a pre-registered margin at p < 0.01 |

The proponents should be invited to run GDTAAA itself on the blinded inputs. The commit-reveal hashes make that possible in public without anyone needing to trust anyone else.

The outcomes would read clearly. A pooled AUC indistinguishable from the time-shifted null would retire the 29°S circle. So would the absence of any dose-response with the predicted physical coupling. An AUC of 0.6 or more that survives blinding and controls would overturn standard radar physics and justify searching the circle.

One limit should be stated in advance. adsb.lol's coverage is mostly over land and near coasts. The test validates the detector there, and the transfer test is what checks it at ocean-like density.

## A seven-step plan built entirely on free data

The plan runs three tracks. The core reanalysis is steps 1 to 5. A fast falsification sprint (step 6) runs in parallel and is time-critical, because Ocean Infinity's calm-season window is open now. The WSPR blind test (step 7) is an optional public-good side project. Effort estimates are this research's judgement for one experienced analyst.

**Step 1: rebuild the arcs and pre-register predictions (a few days).** Compute the arcs from the public log rather than a fitted circle. Then produce one prediction table that every later step reuses:

- for each candidate end point (IG hotspot, CSIRO, WSPR, Java, the 306° and 247° crossings, and a 1° grid along the arc), compute arrival times at every open sensor;
- for each, give the position at each satellite image time;
- for each, give the WSPR link crossings.

Publishing this table before opening any waveform or image turns every later test into a genuine prediction.

**Step 2: build an open path ensemble (two to four weeks).** Run MH370Solver for every mode family:

- LNAV great circle;
- true and magnetic track;
- true and magnetic heading;
- Mach and long-range-cruise speed schedules;
- final-major-turn times from 18:25 to 18:41.

Run each under both ERA5 and GDAS winds. Publish the BTO/BFO residuals, the likelihood for each mode and the resulting arc-crossing probability, and benchmark them against UGIB and the DSTG book. The gate: if no mode family beats the others by a meaningful likelihood ratio, say so, and carry the mixture forward rather than the favourite.

**Step 3: settle the end of flight (one to two weeks).** Reproduce Holland's descent bounds from the public BFOs and his published oscillator warm-up envelope. Test whether Lyne's model is physically consistent. Combine the result with the Boeing end-of-flight simulations Iannello published. The output is a probability of a glide, which directly sets the corridor width.

**Step 4: run a joint drift inversion (one to three months).** First, build a versioned debris table from the Malaysian safety investigation report, the ATSB reports and Godfrey's 2021 catalogue. For each item it should record find date and place, reporting delay, assessment class and barnacle data.

Then seed forward ensembles every 0.25° along the arc on 8 March 2014. Force them with at least three current products plus ERA5 winds, using two leeway classes: flaperon-like and generic. Score each start point on:

- beaching, detection and reporting probabilities;
- the absence of finds on the Western Australian coast;
- the null result of the 2014 aerial search;
- an optional isotope-temperature term built from the Herbert data.

The gate: report the north-versus-south likelihood ratio together with its spread across ocean reanalyses. A result that flips between products is a finding, not a failure.

**Step 5: fuse the evidence and discount searched ground (two to four weeks).**

1. Combine steps 2–4 into a posterior over arc-relative cells.
2. Multiply each cell by one minus its search coverage times detection probability.
3. Build coverage for 2014–17 from Geoscience Australia's released bathymetry and the 2022 data review.
4. Build coverage for 2018 from Malaysia's weekly operational updates.
5. Build approximate coverage for 2025–26 from published vessel tracking.

Publish the ranked list of unsearched cells, with code, before the next mobilisation. This research found no open, reproducible posterior that includes the 2018 and 2025–26 null results.

**Step 6: run the acoustic and seismic falsification sprint (one to three weeks, starting now).** Five tasks:

1. Locate the Java event and estimate its magnitude from about 15 open stations, against the M4.1 earthquake at 23:52.
2. Vet the Perth Canyon burst at 00:53:11–00:53:25 against the same windows on 14 control days and against whale-call templates.
3. Attribute the Kimberley transient at 01:32:56.
4. Check the Amsterdam Island seismometer at about 00:24 for the Stead 247° prediction.
5. Look for a Carlsberg Ridge T-phase at Diego Garcia and Rodrigues between about 00:55 and 01:20.

**Step 7: run the WSPR blind test (three to six weeks, optional).** Follow the protocol above.

**Optional: a contrail screen (days).** Run pycontrails on ARCO-ERA5 along the step 2 paths at FL330–FL410. The kill rule: if no segment crosses air at 85–95% or more relative humidity over ice, stop before downloading any imagery.

## Only a handful of outcomes would truly shrink the box

Most possible results would re-rank the search rather than shrink it. The table lists the outcomes that would change the area itself.

| Outcome | Effect on the search | How plausible |
|---|---|---|
| Steps 2–3 independently confirm a steep, unpiloted dive and rule out Lyne's controlled descent | Corridor stays at about ±25 NM, roughly a third to a sixth of the ±70–140 NM alternatives. Because 33–39°S at ±25 NM is ~95–98% cleared, probability moves to data holidays and north of 33°S | Moderate |
| Steps 2–3 support a controlled descent | Corridor widens to ±70–140 NM, sending the next search to the southern off-arc zones (UGIB A2, 22,000 km²; A3, ~166,000 km²) | Low to moderate |
| One mode family beats the rest by a strong likelihood ratio, robust to winds | Collapses the satellite-only multi-peak result to a single band about ±1° of latitude wide | Low to moderate; twelve years of analysis have not achieved it |
| The drift inversion excludes 25–30°S across reanalyses and leeways | Removes the largest drift-plausible stretch never searched to full width | Moderate |
| The drift inversion robustly favours 25–30°S | Justifies a full-width northern search: the area grows before it can shrink | Low to moderate |
| The Perth Canyon burst at 00:53 proves impulsive and unique against control days | Pairs with the HA01 306° bearing to give a point fix near 25.7°S 101.4°E | Very low (narrowband signal; conflicts with the BFO-derived dive) |
| The Java event is confirmed as a ~M4 natural earthquake | Deletes the Java site and saves AUV time | High |
| The WSPR blind test returns AUC ≈ 0.5 | Deletes the 2,830 km² WSPR circle from the priority list | High |

The ceiling is clear. The evidence that could turn the arc into a point is not free:

- CTBTO's HA01 and HA08 waveforms, plus the calibration shots along the arc that Kadri proposed ([Kadri 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-60529-1.pdf));
- the oldest flaperon barnacles held in France;
- the unredacted Inmarsat log fields and other aircraft's BFO logs;
- Ocean Infinity's sonar coverage polygons.

Public-data work can still matter. It can sharpen the case for asking for these, and it can tell the operator, before the next vessel sails, which of the remaining cells deserve its days at sea.

## Conclusion

The search has turned the problem inside out. For a decade the open question was where along the 7th arc MH370 crossed. With the near-arc southern band largely cleared, the questions now are whether it glided and whether the north was wrongly dismissed. Unusually, both can be attacked with the public Inmarsat log, ERA5 and open ocean reanalyses alone. That gives independent researchers a real role, though it is not the role the genre has favoured. The highest-value contributions are an open posterior that discounts already-searched ground, and fast falsification of the leftover candidates. The Java event's magnitude, the Perth Canyon coincidence and the WSPR circle can each be resolved in days to weeks, while Ocean Infinity's last 7,429 km² waits for a vessel.

WSPR's lesson reaches well beyond amateur radio. A detector that fires 45% of the time by chance, tuned after the answer was known and never tested blind, will find a track wherever one is drawn. Contrail claims and single-station acoustic picks are open to the same error. The blind-test template here, with pre-registration, hashed labels, time-shifted nulls and decoy tracks, should become the entry fee for any incidental-sensor claim about MH370. Applied consistently, it would shrink the list of competing hypotheses faster than any new dataset is likely to shrink the seabed.
